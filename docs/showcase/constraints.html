Every mark on the paper

A sketch has two annotation layers: the relationships that hold it together, and the measurements that give it numbers. This study draws both, from H/V to symmetric about a vertical axis, and from a linear dimension to the inline editor. Click any card to see its selected state; in the finder, hover a mark to light its companions and geometry, then click and press Backspace.

click a card · selected state open the contract the marks are screen-space, never document state

The grammar

The geometry is the drawing. A glyph is a mark attached to it (a tick, a ring, a square, a chevron); a badge is a small label for a relationship that has no readable geometric mark; a connector is a quiet dashed tie between participants. Marks stay --ink-3 until you touch them. Only the current thing gets the highlighter.

quiet hover selected dimension one relationship = one mark per participant stack along the entity, not in a tower

Constraints

Every constraint in the solver has one mark, one placement rule and one orientation rule. A vertical line does not rotate its V badge; a vertical axis does rotate the symmetric chevrons. Equal ticks and the symmetric connector are the two places where direction matters most.

Symmetric, in any direction

The mark is a connector perpendicular to the axis, with two chevrons pointing toward it. Because the chevrons lie on the connector, they rotate with the axis: a vertical axis gives left/right chevrons, a horizontal axis gives up/down chevrons. A static ⇄ is wrong. The axis is a dash-dot centreline only while the constraint is active.

Measurements

A dimension is extension lines, a dimension line and one upright value. Linear spans use slash ticks; angular and radial leaders use an open arrowhead. The unit lives in the editor, never in the label. Reference dimensions are parentheses and italic: they read the geometry, they do not drive it.

States

The same mark moves from quiet to hover to selected. On a selected line the highlighter is a wash under the geometry; the mark itself stays ink on paper with a marker underline. A conflict is a dashed ghost plus a checked chip; the geometry does not move, and nothing is written until the user confirms.

Finding a constraint

Hover a mark to light the whole constraint: every companion mark and the sketch geometry it relates. Click one to select it; Backspace removes it. The tooltip names the mark, and the popover is the same shape as every other selection. Try it in the sample below.

When they meet

Auto-position owns the first attempt: a mark gets a slot on its entity, then moves outward, then clusters. A cluster expands into a fan; overlapping hit targets open a chooser; a dragged mark pins and keeps a leader. Marks draw above the sketch, below dimension labels, and always carry a paper halo.

The rules in one place

  • Geometry first. Constraint marks are quiet; dimensions are one step louder; the highlighter is only “current”.
  • One mark per participant. Parallel puts a badge on each line; symmetric puts one connector between them.
  • Hover is a preview, click is a selection. Hover lights every companion mark and the participants; click selects the whole constraint; Backspace deletes it.
  • Selection is a wash under the marks, never their ink. A selected line gets the highlighter; its constraints stay ink on paper with a marker underline.
  • A hovered mark explains itself. A small tooltip names the constraint and its participant count; touch uses the selection popover instead.
  • Collision order is auto → cluster → chooser → drag. Position automatically, collapse into a count, choose when hits overlap, and pin only when the first three cannot resolve it.
  • Orientation follows the geometry. Equal ticks are perpendicular to the line; the perpendicular square turns with the corner; symmetric chevrons are perpendicular to the axis.
  • Badges stay upright; single-participant badges sit on the line. A vertical line still says V, not a rotated V. H/V sit at the midpoint with their paper fill interrupting the line; multi-participant badges sit outside.
  • Marks are screen-space. They never scale with zoom; short entities collapse to a cluster.
  • Slot, don’t stack. 30 / 50 / 70 % along a line; a ring on a point; a radial tick on a circle.
  • Clicking a constraint is not the removal path. The applied chip in the selection popover is.
  • A shared point is the coincident mark. Only two distinct point ids need a dot and a ring.
  • Linear dimensions use slashes. Angular and radial leaders use an open arrow.
  • Labels are upright and unit-free. The editor carries the unit; reference values sit in parentheses.
  • Conflict is a ghost. A checked chip and a dashed mark; the geometry does not move.
  • DOF is a readout. The foot says the count; it does not colour individual constraints.

Not in v1

Concentric, arc–arc tangency, symmetric about a point and fixing a line are not in the solver today. When they land they use this grammar: a concentric ring at the shared centre, a tangent diamond at the contact, a connector through the centre point, a pin at the midpoint.

design notes · constraints

The mark belongs to the geometry

The old sketch drew constraints as letters at the midpoint of a line. A line with H + equal + parallel got three letters in a column. Symmetric was a fixed ⇄ on both lines, even when the axis was vertical. This study replaces the column with marks that belong to the thing they constrain.

What this study settles

  • Hover links the whole constraint. Every companion mark and the participants highlight together; the tooltip names the mark. A parallel is one object, not two independent icons.
  • Click selects; Backspace deletes. A constraint mark is a handle. The selection popover shows its name, participants and delete; the applied chip remains the fast remove.
  • Selection is a wash under the marks. A selected line gets the highlighter; its H, tick or ring stays ink on a paper badge with a marker underline, so it never disappears into the same colour.
  • Symmetric rotates with the axis. A connector perpendicular to the axis, with two inward chevrons. Vertical axis → left/right; horizontal axis → up/down.
  • Equal is a tick, not an equals sign. One tick per group; two ticks for a second group. On a circle the tick is radial.
  • H/V stay upright and sit on the line. The line can be vertical; the badge is a label, not a picture of the line. A single-participant badge sits at the midpoint, with its paper fill interrupting the line; multi-participant badges sit outside.
  • Point-on-line and point-on-circle are rings. A shared endpoint is coincident; it does not need a second ring.
  • Fix is a pin. F reads like a label; a pin reads like a lock.
  • Linear spans use slashes; angular and radial leaders use arrows. The arrow says “this line points at the thing it measures”.
  • Reference is punctuation, not colour. Parentheses and italic, so it still reads in a screenshot.
  • The editor carries the unit. Labels are values; the inline editor is where mm / ° and driving/reference live.
  • Collision order is auto → cluster → chooser → drag. A dragged mark pins, keeps a dotted leader, and can be reset. Marks stay above geometry and below dimension labels.

Open questions

  • Are glyphs always visible, or only on hover/selection? The study proposes always-on glyphs, names on hover, LOD clusters when dense.
  • Do dragged mark offsets persist in the document, or are they UI-only first?
  • How should equal groups renumber when one is deleted?
  • Which angle is measured, and can the label flip to the supplementary one?
  • Through-line or leader for a diameter when the circle is small?
  • Does a vertical dimension’s label rotate? The study keeps it upright.
  • Do constraints on projected/reference geometry need a different mark? Not now; the reference line is already dashed.
