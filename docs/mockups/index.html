Sketch first.
Then solids.
Then print.

A CAD tool for the way I actually work: draw a constrained profile, give it thickness, and send it to the printer. The interface is paper and ink, with one highlighter colour — yellow, pink, blue, or green — for the thing that is current. The timeline is the workspace; the sketch is the page. There is no permanent toolbar.

the direction

six rules for the visual system

Typography carries the interface. Feature names, measurements, and states are set as type. Lines separate; boxes are rare. The page should read like an annotated drawing, not a dashboard.
One highlighter, used for state. Yellow, pink, blue, or green — one classic highlighter at a time. The chosen colour means current, selected, or changed. It is never decoration. Everything else is ink on paper, or paper on ink at night.
The timeline is the only permanent structure. No sidebar, no ribbon, no mode switcher. History is the map: selecting a step changes the stage, and editing a step is the same gesture. Every step is named; the name is editable and is the handle you use when repairing history.
Controls appear where the decision is. Tools show up in sketch mode. A dimension editor opens at the dimension. Support choices appear only when starting a sketch. Export is a page because it is the end of the job.
The background is a tool. The sketch grid is the snap grid. Its spacing is set in the footer, and the scale bar shows one cell at the current zoom. Snap can be off; the grid stays as a reference.
One home for every fact. The header holds identity and actions. The timeline holds history, names, and feature parameters. The stage status holds solver or body health. The corners hold tools, selection, and view. Nothing repeats.

open questions

things to settle before this becomes the app

  • Is a flat timeline enough, or do sketches need to nest under the features they drive?
  • Should day be the default, or is night mode the better long-session working surface?
  • How much of the feature inspector belongs in the timeline detail, versus inline on the model?
  • Do projected reference edges need a show/hide control, or is the dashed fixed style enough?
  • Should export freeze a mesh into history, or always derive from the current rebuild state?
  • Is a print-orientation preview in scope, or is “fits the build volume” enough for v1?
  • How do multiple bodies, moving parts, and assemblies enter this timeline later without turning it into a tree?
  • How should pan, zoom, and constraint dragging work on touch and tablet, and what is the keyboard equivalent for each view control?
  • Should the filename own download, copy and rename, or does .cad download deserve a separate action?
  • Should body appearance be editable from any step once the body exists, or only from the body-creating step?

design direction

Paper, ink, one highlighter

The studies are a reaction to form-heavy CAD interfaces: permanent ribbons, panels of buttons that may or may not apply, and a timeline treated as a receipt rather than a workspace.

What stays

  • Sketch → model → STL is the whole product loop.
  • The timeline is the one persistent structure.
  • Day and night are the same system inverted.
  • One highlighter — yellow, pink, blue, or green — marks the current decision.
  • Controls are contextual and appear next to the thing they change.

What goes

  • Permanent toolbars and icon grids.
  • Floating cards for every selection.
  • Decorative colour beyond the single highlighter.
  • Buttons that exist in every mode but only work in one.

Chrome boundaries

  • Study bar — study navigation and design notes. Not part of the app.
  • App header — file identity, the next action, and quick-access appearance.
  • Preview controls — the appearance settings above the hero on this page belong to the design document, not the app.

Type

  • Three families — sans for prose, serif for names and titles, mono for labels, values and controls.
  • Eight app roles — display, name, row, prose, control, value, label, micro. The index keeps a separate editorial layer.
  • Enforced — raw font, size, weight, leading and tracking values are rejected by the audit.
The two themes are a design constraint, not an afterthought: every study must work as day with black ink and as night with white ink.
